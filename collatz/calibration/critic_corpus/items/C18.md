## Claim

It is shown that there is no non-trivial orbit and divergent orbit in the Collatz sequences,
or frequently called 3x + 1 mapping . This proves that the conjecture by Collatz is true.

1.    Elementary lemmas
The Collatz problem of positive integers proposed by L. Collatz in 1937 [1] has many
names such as 3x + 1 problem, Syracuse problem, Hasse algorithm, Hasse-Collatz prob-
lem, Kakutani problem, Ulam problem, hailstone sequence or hailstone numbers. This
problem asks whether the following iteration,

always returns to 1 after finite numbers of iteration for any positive integer x.

Notation. When there is no specific denotation, integers such as k, m, n, p, q, r, s, x, y
and those having subscript index will denote positive integers. Especially x with sub-
script index such as xk will denote odd positive integer.

The following lemmas are elementary but required for the succeeding proofs.

Lemma 1.1. There exists a equivalent representation of the Collatz problem. That
is, there exists the unique nk+1 and xk+1 w.r.t xk at any orbit f k+1 represented by a
equivalent form of (1.1) such that

                               f k+1 : 2nk+1 xk+1 − 3xk = 1,                           (1.2)

where all xk and xk+1 are odd and nk+1 is greater than 0.
Proof. Any even positive integer, e.g. x∗ = 2m x0 (m > 0), results odd integer x0 after
m iterations by the rule of even number of (1.1). Thus it is possible, without losing the
generality, to consider that any orbit starts from odd integer. Any odd integer x0 results
even number by the rule of the odd, and then does odd number again by the rule of the
even such as,
                                     3x0 + 1 = 2n1 x1 ,                              (1.3)
where x1 and n1 are trivially unique. Since x1 is odd again, the same process starts by
the rule of odd number.

the k-th iteration started from a given number x0 . 

Lemma 3.6. The backward mapping h̄(ai ← bi ) ≡ k̄ k (xk ) started from the unique
fixed point 1 generates all the member of the set A.

Proof. The Collatz sequences of any starting number must have the convergent or
divergent orbits only because there is no non-trivial orbit by 2.1.
    It is necessary to prove that the number of cyclic orbit has one to one correspondence
between the forward orbit and the backward one. For one forward orbit represented by
xk = xk+s with s ≥ 1, let’s suppose that there exists more than one backward orbit
which are cyclic and which are distinct each other. Then there must exist a orbit xj
with 0 < j ≤ s which results xi,j+1 , distinct each other, for i = 2p + 1 > 1. But, this
is impossible because the forward mapping is injective. Then 2.1 is true as well for the
backward mapping because for any non-trivial orbit, if it exists in the forward mapping,
there exists one and only one non-trivial orbit in the inverse mapping. Therefore there
exists only convergent or divergent orbit in backward mapping as well.
    Let’s investigate the paths possessed by the divergent and convergent orbits. Let’s
suppose that there exists any positive odd integer x0 which is eventually divergent, then
all the pre-image of the orbits f k (xk ) with k = 0, 2, 3, · · · comes from another fixed
point, the infinity, which is distinct from the fixed point 1. Since the forward mapping
is injective, it is impossible that there exists any pre-image of divergent orbit f k (xk )
simultaneously comes from the infinity and from the fixed point 1. This is same to the
convergent orbits.
    Therefore, the set A must be divided into two sets, Ac and Ad , which denotes the
convergent set and the divergent set, respectively. Trivially all the members of Ac will
converge, and all those of Ad will diverge eventually. Then there exists two distinct sets,
B c and B d , which is the range of Ac and Ad , respectively. There is no meaning whether
some members of the set Y defined in (3.3) are the members of B c or B d , because Y
has no pre-image which can come from the fixed point 1 or the infinity by backward
